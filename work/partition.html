01 / The situation

I was the bottleneck, and I couldn't see the queue.

Most of my building now runs through AI coding agents. On a normal day I have thirty-nine sessions open across six projects: sites, a client platform, an app, and this tool itself. Most of the time they don't need me. When one does, it says so quietly, as one row in a list of chat titles.

A row can't tell you who is stuck, who is waiting on a yes, or who finished an hour ago. So the cost was an agent sitting on a one-word question while I was heads-down somewhere else, and client work stalled behind it.

I was the only person who could unblock them, and the only one paying for the wait. I didn't need a better list. I needed to see the room.

02 / The approach

Turn status into body language.

People are very good at reading a room: a raised hand, an empty chair, someone waiting by the door. So Partition stages the work as an office. Each project is a department, each session is a worker at a desk, and status is how they sit. A glance does what scrolling used to.

I wrote the brief and made the calls. Claude Code built it one phase at a time, stopping after each to show me the change and wait for my go-ahead.

How it developed

Five days, one loop at a time.

Each day added one part of the same loop: notice who needs you, answer them, hand out the next thing, get back to work.

  1. Oct 9The brief and the floor.

    Look, status rules and ground rules written down. Sessions appear as workers at desks.

  2. Oct 10Answer from the floor.

    Click a worker, read the conversation, reply in place. The ones waiting longest come first.

  3. Oct 11Hand out work.

    Assign tasks by department, set what a worker may do without asking, schedule repeats, and get a written summary of the day.

  4. Oct 12A real app.

    Its own window and Dock icon, running at login, updating only when no task it started is still running.

  5. Oct 13Oversight.

    A flat plan of the whole floor and every conversation in a department side by side, plus voice input and @ mentions.

Notice and answer

The ones waiting on you come first.

The panel opens on the faces of whoever is waiting, longest wait first, with what each one wants. Pick one and the floor flies to that worker with the conversation open, so you answer right there and the session picks up where it stopped.

Delegate

Hand out the next thing.

Pick a department, Claude or Codex, and how much the worker may do on its own. In Auto, routine edits just happen and anything risky still asks. Approve a kind of request once with Always allow and it never interrupts you again.

Recover

Errors can't hide in a column.

A session that fails doesn't just turn red. Its worker gets up, leaves the floor and waits in a room by the lift. It is the hardest thing on the floor to miss, which is the point.

03 / Why it works

A playful surface on strict rules.

An office you can walk around is only useful if you trust what it shows. These are the calls that made it something I run real work through, and the reason behind each.

  1. 01One status wins.

    Blocked beats waiting beats working beats shipped. A worker shows one state at a time, so the most urgent thing is always the loudest.

  2. 02Status is posture. Identity is clothing.

    A worker's look never changes with their state, so you learn who someone is by their suit and what they're doing by how they sit.

  3. 03Quiet by default.

    Flat light and lots of empty space. The calm is what lets one raised hand stand out, so anything that moved without meaning something was cut: wandering workers, an overtime alarm, a lift ding.

  4. 04Nothing leaves the machine.

    It reads the agents' own files on the Mac, with no account and no upload, and there is deliberately no "skip all permissions" switch. A tool I'd hand my clients' work to had to be boring about safety.

04 / Results

The work didn't get smaller. It got visible.

Short term: within four days of the first commit it was the tool I work from, running at login. Longer term: I run as many agents as before, but nobody waits on me without my knowing, errors can't hide in a scroll, and the day ends with a written record of what got done.

What I didn't expect

Seeing the room created a new queue.

Once I could see everyone waiting, answering them became the slow part: five raised hands meant five trips across the floor. That is why Oversight exists, a flat plan with a whole department's conversations side by side.

The second surprise was trust. A finished session that still looked busy for half an hour did more damage than a missing feature, because it taught me to doubt the floor. Fixing what the floor shows mattered more than adding to it.

The lesson

Give the hidden cost a shape.

When building gets this fast, the hard question stops being "can we make it" and becomes "should it exist." The answer here was to find the thing quietly costing time, make it readable at a glance, and keep only what earns its place. It's the same way I approach client work.
